    Information metabolism.

    I love learning. I want to know so much about so many things. We have a limit to how much we can take in at one time though. I see it just like our diets, of which there are many. A unifying factor in health and nutrition is that if you eat too much of anything, you'll feel too full, and eventually get fat. Three meals a day, and maybe some snacks. Well, for some of us the snacks add up to eight hours of short form videos about an abundance of topics. We take in far more than we can digest, and it shows up as restlessness, fog, indecision, regurgitation of information, and trouble focusing on what you care about.

    There's no shortcut

    Faster answers and more automation won't fix it. You're one person with one brain, and like any AI, you only have so much room to hold things in.

    Switching has a cost

    Jumping topics every few seconds wears down memory, especially when nothing you take in ever comes back out as an idea of your own.

    An invisible muscle

    We train what we can see: bigger arms, bigger meals, more entertainment. This trains what nobody sees: attention, recall, and holding one thought long enough to use it.

    Two practices to start with.

    You need a pen, some paper and a few minutes. The screen's only job is to hand you over to the page.

    Any time a clip comes back to you

    Catch a fragment

    When something you watched pops back into your head, stop and write it down before it drifts off again.

    • What is it: a face, a phrase, a sound, a joke?
    • Who was in it, and where did it come from?
    • What was it about, and what does it say?
    • Why might it be showing up right now?

    Keep the pages together. Over a week, the fragments start to show you a pattern.

    Once a day, on purpose

    Scroll, then recall

    Pick a time limit and scroll as you normally would. When the timer rings, put the phone face down and write down everything you can remember.

    10:00

    Start the timer here, then open your feed. Come back when it rings.

    Phone face down. Now write, by hand:
    1. Everything you saw, in order, as far as you can remember.
    2. One video in detail: who was in it, what it looked like, what was said.
    3. Which one is still with you, and why it stuck.
    4. How you feel now compared with before you started.

    Instagram brain.

    Why the clips you forgot keep coming back, and what to do with them.

    By Solomon. A first draft, November 2026.

    Lately I've noticed something strange. I'll be washing dishes, or halfway through a sentence, and a face appears in my head. Someone I don't know, mid-laugh, from a video I watched days ago and haven't thought about since. Then a phrase. Then a dance move, a kitchen, a punchline without its joke.

    I've started calling this Instagram brain. Short videos leave fragments behind: faces, actions, phrases, intentions. They get stamped in like slides on a carousel, and they come back on their own schedule, out of context, whenever they like.

    Three meals and a lot of snacks

    Think about how we eat information. Most of us have our three square meals: the news in the morning, work all day, something to watch at night. Then we snack. A few minutes in a queue, a few more in bed, a few more that turn into an hour. For some people the snacks add up to eight hours a day.

    Nobody would eat like that and expect to feel good. With information we do exactly that, then wonder why we feel overloaded and foggy and can't settle on one thing. The input never stops long enough for anything to be digested.

    Fragments with no form

    Here's what interests me about the fragments. Something in each of those videos clearly clicked, or it wouldn't still be in there. A joke landed. Something scared me. Something felt relevant to my own life. But I never went back to it on purpose. It just resurfaces when I didn't ask for it, as humour, horror, insight or noise.

    That's the problem. The fragments have no form. They arrive without context and leave without depth, and depth is what we need to be good at anything: our work, our relationships, our own ideas.

    I don't think watching this stuff is bad. It's fun, and some of it is brilliant. But when it never stops, it becomes background noise, and background noise makes focusing hard.

    You're one human with one brain, and you only have so much room.

    One brain, one context window

    I'm writing this in the age of AI, and the comparison keeps coming up. I dictated the notes for this essay to an AI, which is amazing and a little unsettling. Even the AI has limits. Give it a second idea while it's still working on the first, and it struggles to switch and combine them. It only has so much context to work with.

    So do we. Switching topics every fifteen seconds has a cost, especially when nothing you take in ever comes back out as something you made yourself. You end up with an information gut: full, but not fed.

    There's no shortcut here. More automation and faster answers won't fix it. What fixes it is slower and less glamorous.

    Training the invisible muscle

    We like results we can see. Big arms, a big steak, dessert, a show that makes us laugh right now. We overdo the things that pay off immediately, while the small, invisible things that shape how we think go untrained.

    Writing by hand trains one of those invisible muscles. It's slow enough that you have to choose each word. It makes you recall instead of just receive. And it turns a fragment into a sentence, which is the first step to turning it into a thought.

    Two practices to try

    Catch a fragment. Next time a clip pops back into your head, stop and write it down. What is it? Who was in it? Where did it come from, and what was it about? Then ask why it's showing up now, and let it tell you what it's signalling.

    Scroll, then recall. Set a timer and scroll on purpose. When it rings, put your phone face down and write down everything you remember. Not just the headlines, but what it looked like, who was in it and how it was made. It's surprising how little stays, and what does.

    I believe this kind of conscious attention to what goes into us changes how well we function. That's why I'm starting Put It On Paper now, for myself as much as for anyone.
